Project Overview

As part of the Ad-Astra Autonomous Rover Team, I was responsible for designing and fabricating the rover's science cabinet — a precision-engineered enclosure built to house and protect the rover's scientific instruments during competition. The cabinet needed to secure complex, sensitive equipment under field conditions while staying lightweight and compact enough to fit within the rover's overall chassis envelope. It played a direct role in the successful execution of the team's science tasks in competition.

Development & Technical Challenges

The cabinet was designed end-to-end in SolidWorks Sheet Metal, following DFMA principles from the earliest concept stage. Material selection was a key early decision: 304-grade stainless steel at 0.8 mm thickness for its corrosion resistance, low mass, and compatibility with laser cutting and bending — all critical given the fabrication methods available to the team.

The main design challenge was balancing two competing constraints: maximizing usable internal space for mounting multiple scientific instruments, while keeping the structure rigid and secure enough to survive vibration, handling, and field conditions during competition. This meant iterating on panel layout and mounting points until the cabinet achieved both efficient space utilization and secure, stable instrument mounting — without adding unnecessary weight or complexity to the manufacturing process.

Learning Outcome

I developed proficiency in SolidWorks Sheet Metal — working with bend allowances, K-factors, and flat-pattern generation for precision sheet metal enclosures. I gained practical material knowledge of SS304, evaluating its structural rigidity, corrosion resistance, and durability against the demands of rover operating conditions and the realities of fabrication.

Project Overview

I was responsible for engineering the rover's wheels from the ground up — maximizing mobility, traction, and durability across varied and unpredictable terrain. Rather than relying on traditional rigid wheel construction, I used advanced additive manufacturing techniques — FDM 3D printing and Selective Laser Sintering — paired with Thermoplastic Polyurethane (TPU) tires, allowing wheel stiffness to be structurally tuned through geometry rather than material thickness alone.

Iteration 1 → Iteration 2

Wave Tread Pattern: My first design delivered strong forward thrust, reduced straight-line slip, and improved lateral grip. Its geometry also provided a natural self-cleaning effect. However, testing revealed a critical flaw — uneven point-load distribution caused the wheel to sink more than desired in deep, loose sand.

Transverse Rib Pattern: To resolve the sinkage problem, I redesigned the tread into wide transverse ribs optimized for flotation and propulsion efficiency in fine soils. Widening the terrain contact area distributed the rover's weight more uniformly, drastically cutting sinkage. The wide ribs also mechanically scooped and pushed soil backward as the wheel rotated, generating higher forward reaction force.

Learning Outcome

Extensive hands-on DFAM experience — bridging complex CAD models with physical production using flexible TPU filaments. I developed proficiency in slicer software, tuning retraction speed and print temperature to solve stringing and poor bed adhesion. I learned to balance TPU's impact absorption and structural flexibility against its fabrication constraints, strategically tuning infill density and geometry to engineer a field-ready rover wheel.

Project Overview

As Mechanical Subsystem Co-Lead for the Adastra student-built autonomous rover, I was responsible for engineering and directing core mechanical assemblies — focusing on maximizing mobility, structural integrity, and reliability across varied terrain. I balanced high-level subsystem leadership with hands-on development, focusing on optimizing physical hardware, managing component weight envelopes, and ensuring seamless integration across the rover's mechanical architecture.

Development & Technical Challenges

Guiding the mechanical subsystem required tackling complex design and manufacturing hurdles through rapid, iterative prototyping. I engineered critical rover assemblies — custom TPU wheels and the SS304 structural science enclosure — maximizing traction, durability, and space utilization while keeping total system weight low. I led design revisions responding to real-world performance failures: modifying tread geometries to eliminate deep-sand sinkage and refining panel layouts to improve structural rigidity against field vibrations.

I also contributed to the development of a customized 19:1 cycloidal gear — translating theoretical engineering principles and material selection into practical, hands-on R&D testing and manufacturing processes.

Leadership & Outcome

Coordinated mechanical development cycles, aligned design choices with broader team requirements, and ensured functional reliability under field conditions. The team achieved First Runner-Up at IRC 2026 — validating the rover as a competitive system against international university teams, with our being the sole team representing India at URC 2025 in Utah.

Project Overview

I designed a Microbial Fuel Cell (MFC) — a bio-electrochemical system that generates usable electricity by harnessing the metabolic activity of bacteria digesting agricultural waste. The product converts an otherwise low-value waste stream into a continuous, low-power electrical current through a closed-loop, three-chamber system.

Development & Technical Challenges

The core engineering challenge was designing a structure that kept three distinct chemical environments working together without contaminating one another. The anode chamber is sealed and kept oxygen-free, where exoelectrogenic bacteria digest agricultural waste, stripping electrons from organic matter. A Proton Exchange Membrane acts as a selective gatekeeper, allowing protons to pass while blocking oxygen. The cathode chamber completes the circuit, producing only water as a byproduct.

The main design difficulty was maintaining strict fluid and gas isolation between the anaerobic and aerobic sides while positioning electrodes close enough for efficient electron harvesting — two requirements that pull the design in opposite directions.

Learning Outcome

This project let me bridge biology and engineering — translating micro-organism metabolism into a predictable, usable electrical current. I gained practical experience in structural architecture and environmental isolation, integrating an anaerobic anode chamber, a PEM, and an aerobic cathode chamber into a single cohesive unit.

Project Overview

I engineered an Improved Onion Storage System designed to drastically reduce post-harvest losses caused by sprouting, rotting, and moisture loss. The core goal was to replace passive storage with a highly regulated, controlled-environment system that actively extends shelf life.

Development & Technical Challenges

The system was built around precise environmental control: configured to maintain optimal curing temperatures (25–30°C) before transitioning to long-term storage (0–2°C), while strictly managing relative humidity at 65–70% to inhibit fungal growth. A forced-air ventilation system continuously circulates air through the storage structures, actively removing excess heat and respiratory moisture — extending shelf life to 8 months.

On the fabrication side, I modelled structural panels in SolidWorks, designing interlocking slot joints specifically for laser-cutting. Working with MDF required calibrating the laser's kerf carefully to achieve accurate press-fit tolerances between panels.

Learning Outcome

I gained hands-on experience designing interlocking laser-cut structural panels. Working with MDF taught me to evaluate its machinability and flat structural integrity while calibrating fabrication tolerances for a clean press-fit — a materials and manufacturing challenge distinct from the SS304 and TPU work in my other projects.
